To find hidden cameras in a hotel room, run three checks. First, inspect anything with a sightline to the bed or bathroom, especially the alarm clock, smoke detector, and USB chargers, using a flashlight to catch the glint of a lens. Second, turn off the lights and sweep the room with your phone camera to spot infrared LEDs, which appear as glowing dots on screen. Third, scan for wireless signals, starting with Bluetooth, since shared hotel Wi-Fi often blocks guest-to-guest network scans.

Hidden cameras in hotel rooms are rare, but they are not hypothetical. Guests have found them in smoke detectors, clock radios, and wall chargers. A thorough sweep takes less than ten minutes with tools you already carry. This guide walks through the full process, then covers the part most articles skip: what to do, and who to call, if you find something.

Why a hotel room is a different problem than a rental

Most hidden camera advice is written for vacation rentals, where the person most likely to plant a device is the owner. Hotels flip that logic. A hotel chain has no interest in filming you, but a room passes through many hands: housekeeping, maintenance, contractors, and every previous guest. A camera planted in a hotel room is almost always the work of an individual, hidden recently, and usually battery powered or plugged into an outlet.

That changes your search in two ways. First, you are looking for objects added to the room, not built into it. A room with two smoke detectors, or a spare "charger" left in the outlet by the bed, is a red flag. Second, the network environment is different. In an Airbnb, the Wi-Fi belongs to the host and a network scan shows you everything on it, a scenario we cover in our guide to finding hidden cameras in an Airbnb. In a hotel, the Wi-Fi is shared by hundreds of guests, which requires the different approach covered below.

Where cameras hide in hotel rooms

Cameras need three things: power, concealment, and a view, which narrows the search dramatically. Before you inspect anything, sit on the bed and note every object that faces you, then do the same from the bathroom doorway. Those sightlines are your priority list.

Step 1: the flashlight sweep

Every camera needs a lens, and a lens reflects light differently than the plastic around it. Turn off the room lights, close the curtains, and turn on your phone flashlight. Hold it close to your eyes and sweep it slowly across each priority object from the table above. A camera lens throws back a small, sharp glint, usually blue or purple tinted, that stays fixed as you move the light. Ordinary plastic and glass produce broad, soft reflections that shift with your angle.

Work the sightlines first: nightstand, clock, outlets near the bed, then the bathroom. Get close, since a pinhole lens is one to two millimeters across and easy to miss from a distance. Run your hand over suspicious surfaces too; a drilled hole or fake screw head often feels wrong before it looks wrong.

Step 2: the infrared check

Most hidden cameras record in the dark using infrared LEDs that are invisible to your eyes but visible to a phone camera sensor. With the room as dark as you can make it, open your camera app and pan slowly around while watching the screen. Active IR illuminators appear as glowing white or purple dots, often in a small ring pattern.

Two practical notes. On iPhones, the rear camera has a stronger IR filter than the front camera, so use selfie mode for this check. And test your setup first: point the phone at a TV remote and press a button. If the remote's tip flashes on screen, your camera sees infrared and the sweep is valid.

Step 3: scan the wireless environment

Wireless cameras announce themselves. They pair over Bluetooth for setup, stream over Wi-Fi, or both, and those signals are detectable even when the hardware is hidden.

Start with Bluetooth, because it works the same in a hotel as anywhere else. A Bluetooth scan lists every broadcasting device within range, including cheap spy cameras, trackers, and devices in setup mode. In a hotel you will see neighbors' headphones and TVs, so watch signal strength rather than names: an unnamed device that gets stronger as you move toward the nightstand is worth a physical search of that spot. Wi-Fi scanning needs a hotel-specific caveat that most guides miss. Scanning the hotel's guest network will show you either hundreds of unrelated devices or, more often, almost nothing, because hotels commonly enable client isolation, which blocks guests' devices from seeing each other. That is a privacy feature, not proof the room is clean. The workaround: many planted cameras never join the hotel Wi-Fi at all and instead broadcast their own network, so check your phone's Wi-Fi list for odd hotspot names with a strong signal, things like "IPC," "CAM," or a string of letters and numbers that appears only in your room. What to do if you find a camera in a hotel

Finding a device turns a privacy exercise into a crime scene, and the order of operations matters.

Do not touch, unplug, or dismantle the device. It carries fingerprints and stored footage, and both matter to an investigation. Instead, block its view: hang a towel over it or move out of its line of sight.

Document everything before anyone else is involved. Photograph the device where it sits, the whole room, and anything that shows the date, and screenshot any scan results.

Call the local police non-emergency line before you call the front desk. This is the step people get backwards. Recording someone in a hotel room or bathroom is a crime in every US state, and police can take custody of the device and its footage. If you notify the hotel first, a well-meaning manager may remove the camera and break the evidence chain, and in the rare case a staff member is involved, you have given them warning.

Then inform the front desk, request a different room on a different floor, and sweep the new room before settling in. Keep your booking records and the names of everyone you speak with. If it is a chain hotel, follow up in writing with corporate guest relations, since franchise front desks do not always escalate, and report it to any third-party booking site you used.

Know what your tools can and cannot do

No single method is complete. The flashlight finds lenses but requires patience. The IR check only catches cameras using infrared at that moment. Wireless scans miss cameras that record to a memory card without transmitting, which is why the physical sweep always comes first. Apps are genuinely useful for the wireless layer, but any app claiming to magically find every camera is overpromising, and we say that as the makers of one. We break down the honest limits in our guide to whether hidden camera detector apps work.

The realistic goal is not certainty, it is coverage. Check the sightlines, sweep with a flashlight, run the IR check, and scan the wireless environment, and you have closed off every practical way a camera operates. It is a ten-minute habit worth keeping.

Frequently asked questions

Can my iPhone really detect hidden cameras?

Yes, in two ways. The camera sensor can pick up the infrared light that most hidden cameras emit in the dark, which appears as a small glowing dot on your screen. Your phone can also scan for the Bluetooth signals and Wi-Fi network activity that wireless cameras produce, which is how apps like RF Signal Detector surface devices you cannot see.

Where are hidden cameras most often found in hotel rooms?

The most common hosts are alarm clocks, smoke detectors, USB chargers and power adapters, and decor items positioned with a direct view of the bed. Bathroom vents, tissue box holders, and hooks on the back of the bathroom door are the most common spots outside the bedroom. Anything with power and a sightline to where you sleep or undress deserves a look.

Is it ever legal for a hotel to put a camera in a guest room?

No. In the United States, recording guests in hotel rooms and bathrooms is illegal in every state because these are spaces with a clear expectation of privacy. Cameras in lobbies, hallways, and parking areas are legal and normal. A camera inside your room is a crime regardless of who placed it.

What should I do if I find a hidden camera in my hotel room?

Do not touch or unplug it. Cover it with a towel or move out of its view, photograph the device and the room from several angles, and call the local police non-emergency line before you tell the front desk. Then request a different room on another floor, keep all booking records, and follow up in writing with the hotel's corporate office.

To find hidden cameras in a bathroom, turn off the lights and sweep every fixture with your phone flashlight, looking for the pinpoint glint of a lens in vents, hooks, air fresheners, bottles, and smoke detectors. Then run the shower hot, since steam fogs a plastic lens cover differently than the wall around it, and scan the Wi-Fi network, since most spy cameras stream their footage.

A bathroom is the one room where the law, and basic decency, say nobody can record you. It is also a small, hard, water-filled room that offers a camera very few places to hide, which means it can be swept thoroughly in about ten minutes. This guide walks through the short list of viable hiding spots, the physical tests that work in a bathroom specifically, and exactly what to do if you find something in a gym, rental, or workplace restroom.

Why bathrooms are the highest-stakes room to check

Surveillance law in the United States mostly turns on one phrase: reasonable expectation of privacy. In a hallway or lobby that expectation is weak, which is why a landlord or gym can legally point cameras at common areas. In a bathroom it is treated as absolute. Every state criminalizes recording where people undress, and many, including California, Alabama, and Massachusetts, name restrooms explicitly, with penalties reaching felony level.

That means there is no gray area to argue about. A camera in a bathroom is a crime, full stop, no matter who owns the building or what the lease says. It is also why bathrooms attract the worst offenders: police cases have involved cameras in stall coat hooks, gym air fresheners, and smoke detectors installed by hosts and employers. If you check only one room anywhere you stay, make it this one.

The short list of places a bathroom camera can actually hide

A bathroom works against the person hiding a camera. There is moisture, there are few outlets, and almost every object has an obvious function. A camera needs three things: a sightline to the shower or toilet, power or a battery, and a disguise. Only a handful of objects satisfy all three.

The darkness test: catch the lens and the infrared glow

Every camera, however well disguised, has one component it cannot hide: a glass lens that reflects light. Close the door, turn off the lights, and sweep your phone flashlight slowly across each spot on the list, watching for a pinpoint glint that stays fixed on the object as you move the light. Tile and chrome throw broad reflections; a lens throws a tiny, tight one, often blue or purple tinted.

While the room is dark, also look for infrared. Night-vision cameras illuminate the room with IR LEDs that are invisible to your eye but visible to a phone sensor, appearing as a faint glowing dot on screen. An IR camera in a dark vent may run its illuminators constantly.

The steam trick

This test is unique to bathrooms, and it is surprisingly effective. Run the shower hot with the door closed and the fan off for a few minutes, then examine the room while everything is fogged. Mirrors and tile fog evenly. A plastic lens cover, a pinhole opening, or a spot warmed by electronics behind it will fog unevenly, stay clear in a small circle, or clear faster than its surroundings.

Watch the mirror as the fog forms and again as it clears. A clear patch that reappears in the same spot can indicate a warm device behind the glass, and the same logic applies to a vent grille or smoke detector housing, since electronics give off heat and heat fights condensation. It is not precise, but it costs nothing and has exposed cameras that survived a visual inspection.

Testing the mirror, and where the fingernail test fails

The classic advice is the fingernail test. Touch your nail to the mirror. On a standard mirror the reflective coating sits behind the glass, so you see a gap between your nail and its reflection. On a two-way mirror the coating is on the front surface, so your nail appears to touch its reflection.

Use the test, but know its limits. Some quality modern mirrors are first-surface mirrors with no gap and no camera behind them, and lighting can make the gap hard to judge. Treat a failed fingernail test as a reason to keep checking, not a verdict. Cup your hands against the glass and shine your flashlight through: if there is a cavity behind a two-way mirror, you can often see into it. Tap the glass and listen; a mirror flush on a wall sounds dull, while one concealing a cavity sounds hollow.

Scan the network, not just the room

Nearly every consumer spy camera sold today is a Wi-Fi camera, because the buyer wants to watch remotely rather than retrieve a memory card from a bathroom. That is a weakness you can exploit without touching a single fixture: scan the local network and look at every connected device. Camera hardware often identifies itself through its manufacturer even when the device name is blank, and you can run any device's MAC address through our free hidden camera device checker, which flags manufacturers known to make camera modules.

A network scan will not catch a camera recording to a memory card with its radio off, which is why the physical sweep above still matters. But combined, the two approaches cover both kinds of device: the lens test finds anything with glass, and the network scan finds anything that transmits.

What to do if you find a camera

Your instinct will be to grab it, unplug it, or smash it. Do none of those things. The device is evidence, and it may carry the fingerprints or account credentials that identify who placed it.

Follow this order instead:

  • Do not touch the device. Leave it powered and in place.
  • Step out of its view, then photograph the device, its sightline, and the room from the doorway.
  • Call the police before anyone else. This is a police matter first and a customer service matter second.

Where you found it changes who else you notify. In a gym or store, tell a manager only after police are on the way, and do not hand over the device; in coat-hook cases the perpetrator has sometimes been an insider. In a vacation rental, report to the platform after filing the police report, and request a refund and relocation in writing. In a workplace, report to HR in writing after police; employers have no bathroom surveillance rights anywhere in the US, and the same goes for landlords, where most states let you break a lease over it. One last point if something already happened: you did nothing wrong by not spotting it sooner. These devices are built to defeat casual observation, and the person who hid it committed the crime, not the person it recorded.

Frequently asked questions

Is a camera in a bathroom ever legal?

No. Bathrooms carry an absolute expectation of privacy in every US state, so recording inside one is illegal regardless of who owns the property. A landlord, gym, or employer may legally record some common areas, but that permission ends at the bathroom door. Penalties range up to felony charges in many states.

How does the fingernail test for two-way mirrors work?

Touch your fingernail to the mirror glass. On a normal back-silvered mirror there is a visible gap between your nail and its reflection. On a first-surface or two-way mirror the nail appears to touch its reflection. The test is unreliable on modern mirrors, so treat it as a first check and confirm by shining a flashlight against the glass or looking behind the mirror.

Can my phone really detect hidden cameras in a bathroom?

Yes, within limits. A phone camera can pick up the infrared glow that night-vision cameras emit in a dark room, and apps can scan the Wi-Fi network for streaming devices and flag known camera hardware. A phone cannot detect a camera that is powered off and recording nothing, which is why a physical inspection of the room still matters.

What should I do if I find a hidden camera in a gym or rental bathroom?

Do not touch or unplug it. Photograph the device and its position, leave the room clothed, and call the police from outside, since bathroom recording is a crime everywhere in the US. Report to management or the platform only after police are involved, and keep copies of every photo and message.

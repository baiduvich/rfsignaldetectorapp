To detect listening devices, combine an electronic scan with a physical search. Use your phone to scan for Bluetooth and Wi-Fi transmitters, check the local network for unknown devices, then physically inspect power outlets, chargers, smoke detectors, and anything new or recently moved. Voice recorders that do not transmit can only be found by hand, so no single scan is ever enough.

Worrying that someone is listening to your conversations is stressful, but finding out is a solvable problem. Most consumer-grade bugs are cheap devices sold openly online, and each type has a known weakness. This guide explains the four kinds of listening devices you are realistically dealing with, which detection method actually works for each one, and the physical sweep order professionals use, all in a check you can finish in under an hour.

Know what you are looking for: the four types of bugs

"Listening device" covers four very different technologies, and each one is found a different way. A method that catches one type can be completely blind to another, which is why so much online advice fails people.

Bug typeHow it sends audioHow to detect it
GSM / cellular bugContains a SIM card and streams live audio over the mobile network, like a hidden phone that auto-answersRF detection and physical search. Phone scanners cannot see it directly, but it needs constant power, which narrows hiding spots
Wi-Fi bugJoins a Wi-Fi network, or creates its own, and streams audio over the internetNetwork scan. It shows up as an unknown device on the Wi-Fi network, or as an unfamiliar network name nearby
Bluetooth bugTransmits to a phone or receiver within roughly 10 to 100 metersBluetooth scan. It advertises itself constantly and signal strength rises as you walk toward it
Voice-activated recorderNothing. It stores audio to internal memory and must be physically retrieved laterPhysical search only. It emits no signal at all, so no scanner of any kind will find it

Three of the four types transmit, and transmissions can be detected. The fourth is silent, which is why every serious sweep ends with your hands and eyes, not an app.

Start with the electronic scan

Do the electronic pass first. It is fast, and it tells you whether anything nearby is transmitting right now.

Scan for Bluetooth transmitters

Bluetooth bugs and trackers announce themselves continuously so their owner's phone can find them. A Bluetooth scan lists every advertising device nearby, and the trick is to identify what each one is. Your headphones, TV, and smartwatch will all appear. What you are looking for is a device you cannot account for, especially one whose signal gets stronger in a specific corner of the room. The same scan is how you catch an AirTag or similar tracker planted in a bag or car, which we cover in detail in our guide to finding an AirTag that is tracking you.

Scan the Wi-Fi network

A Wi-Fi bug has to join a network to send audio out, and joining a network makes it visible. List every device connected to the local network and account for each one: your phones, laptops, TV, printer, thermostat. An entry you cannot explain, particularly one with a generic manufacturer name from a company you have never bought anything from, deserves investigation. In a rental or hotel this is even more powerful, because you should recognize almost nothing as yours except your own devices. Know what the phone scan cannot see

Be honest about the limits. A phone cannot detect a GSM bug's cellular transmission, and it cannot detect a recorder that transmits nothing. Apps that claim to find "all bugs" with a magnetometer are overpromising, and we explain why in our article on whether hidden camera detector apps work. The electronic scan reliably covers Bluetooth and Wi-Fi devices, which are the most common consumer bugs, and that is exactly what it should be used for. The rest is physical work.

The physical sweep: where and in what order

Professionals do not wander a room randomly. They sweep by power, then by position, then by novelty. Here is that order, adapted for a home.

1. Powered locations first

A bug that streams audio around the clock needs constant power. Battery-only bugs last days at most, so long-term surveillance almost always taps mains power. Check, in order: USB chargers and power adapters plugged into outlets, power strips and extension cords, outlets and light switch plates themselves, smoke detectors, ceiling lights and lamps, routers and cable boxes, and any always-plugged appliance like a clock or air purifier. Pick each item up. A charger with a bug inside is often heavier than a genuine one, and a transmitting device is frequently warm even when nothing is plugged into it. Warmth without a job to do is a real signal.

2. Line-of-sound positions

Microphones need to be near where conversation happens. Prioritize the couch area, the bed, the desk where you take calls, and the dining table. Check the underside of furniture, behind headboards, inside decorative objects on shelves at head height, and along the top edges of curtains and frames. A smoke detector directly above a seating area deserves a close look, since detector housings are a favorite shell for both microphones and cameras.

3. Anything new or moved

This is the step people skip and it finds the most devices. Planting a bug requires access, so think about who has been in the space recently: contractors, cleaners, an ex with a key, a landlord visit. Then look for what changed. A gift you did not ask for, a new pen holder on your desk, a clock that appeared after a visit, an outlet plate with fresh scratches on the screws, dust rings that show an object was lifted and put back slightly off. New plus powered plus near conversation is the highest-risk combination there is.

4. The silent recorder problem

A voice-activated recorder is the cheapest bug on the market, often under $40, and no scanner will ever find it because it transmits nothing. The good news is that its weakness is built in: someone has to come back for it. That means it will be somewhere accessible without tools, not sealed inside a wall. Check drawers, under seat cushions, inside bags and coat pockets, on top of tall furniture, and in vehicles under seats and in door pockets.

Warning signs that justify a sweep

You do not need proof to justify checking, but some patterns deserve attention. People repeating details of private conversations they had no way of knowing. Objects that are warm when idle. Clicking on calls that only happens in one room. New items appearing after someone had access to your space. None of these alone proves anything, but two or three together after a specific person had access is worth taking seriously. When to hire a professional, and what it costs

A TSCM (technical surveillance countermeasures) professional makes sense in three situations: a legal dispute where evidence handling matters, such as a divorce or custody case; a business context where trade secrets are at stake; or a case where you have strong signs of surveillance but your own sweep found nothing. Professionals bring equipment a consumer cannot, including spectrum analyzers that see GSM transmissions and non-linear junction detectors that find electronics even when powered off. That gear costs tens of thousands of dollars, which is why a residential sweep typically runs $500 to $2,500 in the US, and large properties or corporate offices cost more. Be wary of anyone offering a $150 "bug sweep" with a handheld wand, since that is less capable than a careful DIY sweep. And if you do find a device, photograph it in place, do not unplug it, and call the police. Recording someone's private conversations without consent is a crime in every US state, and the device itself is evidence.

A realistic bottom line

Most people who sweep their space find nothing, and that result is worth having. For the rest, the combination described here covers all four bug types: a Bluetooth scan for Bluetooth bugs and trackers, a network scan for Wi-Fi devices, and a physical search for GSM bugs and offline recorders, guided by power, position, and novelty. Do the electronic pass in minutes, do the physical pass once with real attention, and repeat after anyone has unsupervised access. That routine is close to what a professional does on day one, and it costs you an hour.

Frequently asked questions

Can my iPhone detect listening devices?

Yes, partially. An iPhone can scan for Bluetooth and Wi-Fi transmitters, which covers two of the four common bug types, and its camera can help spot lens reflections on combined camera-microphone devices. It cannot detect GSM bugs or offline voice recorders, so pair any phone scan with a physical search.

What does a listening device look like?

Most modern bugs are hidden inside ordinary objects that have a reason to be plugged in: USB chargers, power strips, smoke detectors, clocks, lamps, and pens. A bare bug is typically a small circuit board with a pinhole microphone, often smaller than a matchbox. Assume anything with constant power and a clear line to conversation areas is a candidate.

How do I find a bug that does not transmit?

A voice-activated recorder with no transmitter emits no radio signal, so no scanner will find it. It can only be found physically. Search methodically by zone, check anything new or recently moved, feel for objects that are warm when they should not be, and remember the device must be retrieved eventually, so it will be somewhere accessible.

How much does a professional bug sweep cost?

A professional TSCM sweep of a home or small office typically costs between $500 and $2,500 in the US, and more for large properties. Reputable examiners use spectrum analyzers and non-linear junction detectors that cost tens of thousands of dollars, which is why the service is priced the way it is. It is worth it for legal disputes or business espionage concerns.

An AirTag is the size of a coat button, costs under 30 dollars, and can report your location to someone else for a year on one battery. Apple built anti-stalking protections into iOS, and they work, but they have real delays and real gaps that you should understand before you rely on them. This guide walks through exactly how the alerts behave, how to force a hidden AirTag to reveal itself, where trackers actually get hidden, and what to document so police can identify the owner.

How Apple's anti-stalking alerts actually work

Every AirTag constantly broadcasts a Bluetooth signal so nearby Apple devices can relay its position to its owner. Your iPhone listens to those same broadcasts. When it notices a tag that is separated from its owner and keeps showing up as you move between locations, it displays the alert "AirTag Found Moving With You."

The important part is the timing, because this is where most articles are vague:

  • The iPhone alert has no fixed schedule. It fires after the tag has traveled with you across multiple locations. In practice that can be under an hour of driving around, or the better part of a day if you stay in one place. Staying home all evening usually delays the alert, because "moving with you" is the trigger.
  • The AirTag's own speaker is much slower. A tag separated from its owner is designed to chirp on its own after a random window of 8 to 24 hours. In independent testing by Tom's Guide, the chirp often did not arrive until around the 12 hour mark, played for only about 15 seconds, and then went silent again for hours. It is quiet enough to muffle with a strip of tape or a layer of fabric, and modified "silent" AirTags with the speaker removed are sold openly online.
  • Alerts require a signed-in iPhone. You need iOS 14.5 or later, Bluetooth on, Location Services on, and Find My tracking notifications enabled in Settings, then Notifications, then Tracking Notifications. If any of those are off, you get nothing.

The takeaway: the system is good, but a determined person gets a multi-hour head start, and possibly a permanent one if they muffle the speaker and you miss the notification. That is why knowing how to search manually matters.

Step 1: make the AirTag play a sound

If you received the alert, tap it. Your iPhone opens a screen showing a map of where the tag has been observed with you. From there:

  1. Tap Continue, then tap Play Sound. The AirTag near you will chirp so you can hunt it down. You can replay it as many times as you need.
  2. If Play Sound is grayed out, the tag is either no longer in Bluetooth range or its owner is nearby again. Move to another room and retry, or note the time and try again later.
  3. On an iPhone 11 or newer, you may also see Find Nearby. This uses ultra wideband Precision Finding to show live distance and direction arrows, exactly like finding your own lost AirTag. It is by far the fastest way to walk straight to a hidden tag.

Never got an alert but still suspicious? Open the Find My app, tap the Items tab, then tap Identify Found Item. This runs an on-demand scan for AirTags and Find My trackers near you that are separated from their owner.

Step 2: physically find it from the sound

The chirp is high-pitched and surprisingly hard to localize, especially inside a car. A few field-tested tricks:

  • Turn off music, HVAC fans, and appliances first. Fifteen seconds of chirping goes fast.
  • Cup a hand behind each ear and rotate your head slowly. High frequencies are directional, and this crude trick genuinely helps.
  • In a car, check the spots people actually use: under bumpers, inside the front and rear license plate area, in the tow hitch receiver, inside wheel wells, under seats, in the spare tire well, and in seat-back pockets. If the tag seems attached to the vehicle rather than dropped in a bag, read our full guide on how to detect a GPS tracker on your car, because dedicated GPS units hide in the same places and do not trigger Apple alerts at all.
  • On your person, check coat linings, bag bottom seams, laptop sleeves, umbrella folds, stroller frames, and gifted items like plush toys or water bottles.

An AirTag is a white and silver disc, 1.26 inches across, about the thickness of two stacked quarters. Third-party holders can disguise it as a keyfob, a bike reflector, or a black rubber puck.

Step 3: tap it with NFC and record everything

Once you have the tag in hand, do not pull the battery yet. First, hold the top of your iPhone, or any NFC-capable Android phone, against the white side of the AirTag. A notification appears that opens a page at found.apple.com showing:

  • The AirTag's serial number
  • Whether it has been marked lost, and if so, a partial phone number for the owner

Screenshot this page immediately. The serial number is the key piece of evidence. Every AirTag is paired to an Apple ID, and Apple states it will provide the registered owner's account details to law enforcement in response to a subpoena or valid legal request. People have been criminally charged for AirTag stalking based on exactly this chain of evidence.

While you are documenting, also save:

  • Screenshots of the "Found Moving With You" alert and the map of locations where the tag was seen with you
  • Photos of the tag exactly where you found it, before you moved it
  • The dates and times of each alert

A note on your safety

Take this part seriously. If there is any chance the person tracking you is someone dangerous, a current or former partner, or anyone with access to your home or car, do not immediately disable the tag. The moment it goes offline or leaves your location, the owner can see that, and it signals that you found it. That can escalate a dangerous situation. Instead, document everything as described above, contact your local police or, in the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, and let them help you decide the safest timing. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Do not confront the person you suspect.

What if it is not an AirTag? Tile, SmartTag, and clones

AirTags get the headlines, but they are actually the hardest tracker to stalk someone with, precisely because iPhones snitch on them. Other trackers behave differently, and the protections vary a lot.

In 2024 Apple and Google shipped a joint industry standard called Detecting Unwanted Location Trackers. Frequently asked questions

How long before my iPhone warns me about an unknown AirTag?

There is no fixed timer. Your iPhone alerts you once an AirTag that is separated from its owner has moved with you across locations, which can take anywhere from under an hour to most of a day depending on how you travel. The AirTag's own speaker is slower, chiming only 8 to 24 hours after it leaves its owner's range, and only for about 15 seconds at a time.

Can I find an AirTag if I never got an alert?

Yes. Open the Find My app, go to the Items tab, and tap Identify Found Item, or run a manual Bluetooth scan with an app like RF Signal Detector to list every tracker broadcasting near you. Physically checking bags, coat pockets, and your car is still the most reliable backstop.

What information does tapping an AirTag with my phone reveal?

Holding the top of any NFC-capable phone against the white side of an AirTag opens a found.apple.com page showing the tag's serial number and, if it has been marked lost, a partial phone number for the owner. Screenshot that page. Apple can link the serial number to the owner's Apple ID when police submit a legal request.

Should I remove the battery from an AirTag I find on me?

Not immediately if you may be in danger. The moment the tag goes offline, the owner can see it stopped reporting, which tells a stalker they have been discovered. Document it first, contact police, and let them advise whether to disable it, especially in domestic violence situations.

How to detect a GPS tracker on your car

Updated July 25, 2026 · RF Signal Detector team

To detect a GPS tracker on your car, search the vehicle in a fixed order: wheel wells, the underside and frame, front and rear bumpers, the OBD-II port under the dashboard, then under the seats and in the trunk. Pair the physical search with a Bluetooth scan on your phone, which catches AirTags and similar trackers that a visual check can miss. Cellular GPS trackers cannot be detected by a phone app, so the physical inspection is the core of the sweep.

If someone always seems to know where you have been, or a tracker alert has popped up on your phone, it is reasonable to check your car. A thorough sweep does not require professional equipment. It requires knowing what the three types of trackers look like, where each type hides, and which tool finds which type. This guide covers all of it in the order a professional would follow.

Know the three types of car trackers first

People say "GPS tracker" as if it were one thing, but there are three distinct categories, and the right detection method depends on which one you are dealing with.

TypePower sourceHow it reports locationHow you find it
Battery-powered GPS trackerInternal battery, weeks to monthsCellular network (built-in SIM)Physical search, usually magnetic mount on the underside
Hardwired or OBD-II GPS trackerThe car's own powerCellular networkCheck the OBD port and look for spliced wiring behind the dash
Bluetooth tracker (AirTag, Tile, SmartTag)Coin cell, about a yearNearby phones relay its positionBluetooth scan on your phone, then physical search

The distinction matters because a cellular GPS tracker actively transmits to cell towers, exactly like a phone does. Your iPhone does not listen for other devices' cellular transmissions, so no app can spot one directly. A Bluetooth tracker is the opposite. It constantly broadcasts a short-range Bluetooth signal so nearby phones can relay its position, and that same broadcast is what makes it detectable with a Bluetooth scanner. One type hides from your phone, the other announces itself to it.

This is also why Bluetooth trackers have become the cheap stalking tool of choice. A real GPS tracker needs a monthly SIM subscription. An AirTag costs about thirty dollars once, and in a city it updates every time an iPhone drives past.

The physical search, in the order that finds trackers fastest

Do the search in daylight or with a good flashlight, and work through the locations in this order. It goes from the most common hiding spots to the least common, so most trackers turn up in the first ten minutes.

1. Wheel wells and behind the plastic liners

Reach up inside each wheel well and feel along the metal above the tire. Trackers here are magnetic boxes, usually black or dark gray, about the size of a deck of cards or a bar of soap, often in a rubber weatherproof case. Push gently on the plastic wheel-well liners too. A tracker can sit between the liner and the body.

2. The underside and frame

Use your phone flashlight and look under the car along the frame rails, around the fuel tank, and near the exhaust heat shields. You are looking for anything box-shaped that is not factory hardware. Factory components are bolted, dusty, and match the grime around them. A planted tracker is often cleaner than everything near it, which is a genuine tell.

3. Front and rear bumpers

Modern bumpers are plastic shells with large hollow cavities behind them. Feel inside the gaps from below, and check behind the license plates. The tow hook covers and the recess around them are also worth a look.

4. The OBD-II port

The onboard diagnostics port sits under the dashboard on the driver's side, usually within reach of your left knee. Anything plugged into it that you did not put there is a finding. OBD trackers are matchbox-sized, need no battery, and run forever on the car's power. Some use a short extension cable so the tracker itself can be tucked deeper behind the dash, so follow any cable leaving the port.

5. Cabin and trunk

Check under both front seats, inside seat-back pockets, in the glove box, under the dashboard, and in the center console. In the trunk, lift the floor panel and check around the spare tire and side compartments. This is where Bluetooth trackers usually live. An AirTag is the size of a large coin, so also run your hand into seat crevices and under floor mats.

6. Hardwired installs

If the car was bought used or has been out of your control for a service visit, look for non-factory wiring behind the dash and under the steering column: mismatched electrical tape, spliced wires, or a small module zip-tied to an existing harness. This is the rarest scenario for private stalking and the most common for dealer and fleet trackers.

Run a Bluetooth scan to catch what your eyes miss

A physical search can miss a coin-sized tracker dropped inside a seat fold or taped inside a bumper cavity. This is where your phone genuinely helps, because every Bluetooth tracker must broadcast to work. Scan from inside the car in a spot away from home, like a parking lot, so your own gadgets and neighbors' devices do not flood the results. Any tracker that shows a strong signal in the car, and is still there after you drive somewhere else, is physically with the vehicle.

What a phone app honestly can and cannot do here

Being clear about limits matters more in this topic than almost any other. A phone can detect Bluetooth trackers because they broadcast on a protocol the phone already speaks. A phone cannot directly detect a cellular GPS tracker, because that device talks to cell towers on licensed frequencies your phone's hardware does not monitor. It also cannot detect a passive GPS logger, a rarer device that records location to memory and transmits nothing at all.

So the honest formula is: physical search for GPS and logger types, Bluetooth scan for AirTag types, and a dedicated RF sweeper or a professional TSCM inspection if the stakes are high, for example in an active legal dispute. Some trackers only transmit while the vehicle moves, which is why professionals sweep with the car in motion. Found something? Do this before you rip it out

Photograph the device exactly where it sits, including a wide shot showing its position on the car. Note the date and location. If you are dealing with a domestic dispute, a stalker, or anything that might reach a courtroom, call your local police non-emergency line before removing it. A tracker in place is evidence. A tracker in your junk drawer is just a gadget someone can deny owning.

On the legal question: in most US states it is illegal to place a tracker on a vehicle you do not own or co-own, and many states prosecute it under stalking statutes. The gray areas are real, though. A co-owner or spouse on the title, an employer tracking a company car, or a lender with a tracking clause in the finance agreement may be acting legally. That is why the police conversation is worth having, because who owns the car and who planted the device both matter.

Also think about where the tracking data would be going. If the person who planted a tracker has been in your home, a broader sweep is sensible. How often should you check?

One careful sweep when suspicion first arises is enough, plus a quick repeat after any event that gave someone access to the car: a breakup, a service visit, or lending the car out. Battery trackers die in weeks to months, so persistent tracking requires someone to physically return to your car, and each return is a chance to catch a fresh device. A five-minute check of wheel wells, the OBD port, and under the seats, plus a Bluetooth scan from the driver's seat, covers the realistic threats.

Frequently asked questions

Can my phone detect a GPS tracker on my car?

Your phone can detect Bluetooth trackers like AirTags and Tiles, because those broadcast a Bluetooth signal your iPhone can pick up. It cannot directly detect cellular GPS trackers, which transmit over mobile networks your phone does not scan. For those, a systematic physical search is the reliable method, with a dedicated RF sweeper as a backup.

Where are GPS trackers usually hidden on a car?

The most common spots are magnetic mounts in the wheel wells and along the frame under the car, inside or behind the bumpers, plugged into the OBD-II port under the dashboard, and inside the cabin under seats, in the glove box, or in trunk compartments. Battery-powered magnetic units favor the underside, while hardwired units hide behind the dash.

Is it illegal for someone to put a GPS tracker on my car?

In most US states, placing a tracker on a vehicle you do not own or co-own without consent is illegal, and many states treat it as a form of stalking. Rules differ for co-owned vehicles, employer fleet cars, and vehicles financed with tracking clauses. If you find a tracker you did not consent to, photograph it in place and contact local police before removing it.

How long does a hidden GPS tracker battery last?

Battery-powered magnetic trackers typically last from about two weeks to several months depending on how often they report location. Hardwired and OBD-II trackers draw power from the car itself, so they run indefinitely. That is why a physical check of the OBD port and wiring should be part of every sweep, not just a search for magnetic boxes.

Signs your house is bugged: what's real and what's a myth

Updated August 9, 2026 · RF Signal Detector team

The reliable signs your house is bugged are physical and digital, not audible: evidence of entry, objects moved or newly appeared, disturbed outlet plates or drywall dust, unknown devices on your Wi-Fi, unfamiliar Bluetooth devices that never leave, and someone knowing things you only said at home. Phone static, battery drain, and radio interference are outdated indicators. You can verify most of this yourself in an afternoon with a phone scan and a methodical room search.

If you searched this phrase, you are probably not being dramatic. Something specific happened. Someone quoted a conversation they should not have heard, or an ex still has a key, or a gadget appeared in your living room that you do not remember buying. The good news is that this question has a factual answer, and you can get most of the way there yourself. This guide separates the signs that actually indicate a listening device or hidden camera from the ones that articles keep repeating even though they stopped being meaningful two decades ago.

First, the honest odds

Most people who worry their house is bugged are not being surveilled. Covert devices require someone with access to your home, a motive strong enough to risk criminal charges, and enough technical interest to plant and maintain equipment. That combination is rare. If no one in your life fits that description, a single odd noise or a glitchy phone is almost certainly just an odd noise or a glitchy phone.

But "rare" is not "never." Consumer surveillance gear is cheap, sold openly, and disguised as chargers, smoke detectors, and clocks. When bugging does happen, it is almost always personal, and the signs below are how it gets caught.

The real signs, ranked by how much they mean

1. Evidence someone entered your home

Every planted device starts with access. Scratches around a lock, a window latch left different from how you keep it, a door that was deadbolted when you always use the knob lock, or a neighbor mentioning a "maintenance visit" you never scheduled. Entry evidence is the foundation sign. Without access, there is no bug.

2. Objects that moved, or objects that are new

A smoke detector that sits at a slightly different angle. A clock repositioned so it faces the couch instead of the bed. A power strip you do not remember buying. Hidden cameras need a sight line and microphones need proximity to conversation, so planted devices end up aimed at where you talk and gather. If something in your living room or bedroom changed position or appeared without explanation, take it seriously enough to inspect it.

3. Gifts of electronics from someone you are in conflict with

A digital photo frame from an ex during a custody dispute. A "smart speaker" from a business partner you are suing. Gifted electronics are the classic delivery method for a bug because you plug them in voluntarily and give them permanent power and a good spot in the room. This does not mean every gift is compromised. It means a powered gift from an active adversary deserves inspection before it lives in your home.

4. Unknown devices on your Wi-Fi network

Most consumer hidden cameras stream over Wi-Fi, and many attach to whatever network is available, including yours. A device on your network that you cannot match to a phone, laptop, TV, or appliance you own is a concrete, checkable lead. Our guide on how to scan your Wi-Fi for hidden cameras walks through identifying every device on the list and what camera manufacturers' network names look like.

5. Unfamiliar Bluetooth devices that persist

Bluetooth devices from neighbors and passing cars come and go. A device that appears in every scan, at strong signal, at all hours, is physically inside or very near your home. Trackers like AirTags also show up this way. If a strong unknown signal never leaves, it has an address, and that address might be your bookshelf.

6. Drywall dust or disturbed plates

Wired and in-wall devices leave installation traces: a fine line of white dust along a baseboard, outlet or switch plates with paint-scarred screw heads or slight misalignment, a fresh patch of paint that does not quite match. Professional sweep teams check outlet plates early for exactly this reason. Plates get removed to hide devices in the wall cavity behind them.

7. Someone knows things they should not know

This is the sign that sends most people searching in the first place, and it is legitimate, with a caveat. Once or twice can be coincidence, a mutual friend, or a lucky guess. A repeated pattern where a specific person echoes details you only ever said out loud inside your home is different. Write down each incident: what was said, where you said it, who repeated it back. If a pattern points to one room, that room is where you search first.

Why the classic myth signs no longer work

Most "is your house bugged" articles still lead with three signs inherited from the analog era, and it is worth understanding why they went stale.

Clicking and static on phone calls mattered when phones were copper landlines and a wiretap was a physical clamp on the wire that could introduce noise. Your cell phone does not have a wire to clamp. Its calls are encrypted digital streams, and a microphone hidden in your bookshelf has no way to add static to them. Odd call audio today means network congestion, not surveillance.

Fast battery drain gets repeated because it sounds plausible, but a bug in your house is a separate piece of hardware with its own battery or wall power. It does not draw a single milliamp from your phone. Battery drain comes from apps, screen time, and battery age.

Radio and TV interference was a real tell for crude analog FM transmitters in the 1980s. Modern devices transmit clean digital signals over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or cellular, in bands your FM radio never touches. A buzzing speaker is a grounding issue, not a bug.

The pattern is simple: old bugs leaked analog noise, modern bugs leak digital network presence. That is why the productive place to look moved from your radio dial to your device scan.

Who actually gets bugged

A realistic threat model keeps the search grounded. Documented cases of residential bugging cluster into a few situations: separations, divorces, and custody disputes, where an ex wants leverage or control; landlord and roommate situations, where someone has legitimate keys and illegitimate curiosity; small business and partnership disputes, where conversations have money attached; and stalking or harassment cases that escalate. Short-term rentals are their own category with a different dynamic, since the owner controls the space before you arrive.

What these share is a specific person with access and motive. Strangers do not bug random houses. If you cannot name a candidate, your baseline risk is very low, and one weird sign deserves a calm check rather than a spiral. If you can name a candidate, especially one with a key, the verification sequence below is worth doing properly.

How to actually verify: a four-layer sequence

Run the layers in order. Each one either clears a category of device or hands you a physical lead.

Layer 1: scan your Wi-Fi network

List every device connected to your network and account for each one. Phones, laptops, TV, thermostat, printer. Anything left over gets investigated, not ignored. If a device name is cryptic, look up its manufacturer, and use our free hidden camera device checker to test whether the name matches known camera brands.

Layer 2: scan for Bluetooth devices

Scan several times across a day. Ignore signals that come and go. Flag anything unfamiliar that persists at strong signal, then use signal strength to walk toward it. Stronger means closer.

Layer 3: check for camera lenses optically

Darken each room and use an infrared lens check, which spots the IR illuminators night-vision cameras use and the glint of lens glass. This catches cameras that record locally and never touch a network. Knowing what a hidden camera actually looks like helps here, since most are disguised as everyday objects.

Layer 4: search the priority spots physically

Devices need power and coverage, so search where those overlap: outlets and power strips, smoke detectors, anything on a shelf facing the bed or couch, routers, clocks, and any gifted electronics. Unplug what you cannot identify. A device without power is a solved problem. For microphone-specific spots and techniques, see our full guide on how to detect listening devices.

If all four layers come back clean, that is a meaningful result. You have checked networked devices, local-recording cameras, and the physical hiding spots, which covers the overwhelming majority of consumer surveillance gear.

When a professional sweep is worth the money

A professional TSCM sweep, short for technical surveillance countermeasures, uses spectrum analyzers, thermal imaging, and non-linear junction detectors that can find devices even when they are powered off. For an average US home, expect to pay roughly $1,500 to $6,000 depending on square footage and whether vehicles and phones are included. Be skeptical of $300 "sweeps," which typically use the same consumer-grade detectors you could buy yourself.

That price is justified in specific situations: active litigation or a custody battle where recordings could surface as leverage, a business dispute involving real money, a stalking case where law enforcement is already involved, or when your own scans found something you cannot explain and you need documentation. It is not the right first step for a general uneasy feeling. Run the phone-based sequence first. Either it resolves the worry for free, or it gives the sweep team concrete findings to start from, which makes their work faster and your money better spent.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most reliable signs your house is bugged?

The strongest signs are physical and behavioral: evidence someone entered your home, objects that moved or appeared without explanation, disturbed outlet plates or fine drywall dust, unknown devices on your Wi-Fi network, and someone repeatedly knowing private things you only said at home. One sign alone proves little. Two or three together, plus a person with access and a motive, is worth investigating.

Is phone battery drain or call static a sign of bugging?

No, not anymore. Battery drain almost always comes from apps, battery age, or background activity, and static or clicking was a landline-era indicator. Modern bugs are digital, transmit over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or cellular, and do not interact with your phone calls or radio at all. Treat these as myths and focus on physical and network evidence instead.

How can I check if my house is bugged without hiring anyone?

Do a layered check: scan your Wi-Fi network and identify every connected device, scan for Bluetooth devices that persist in your home, darken each room and check for infrared light from camera lenses, then physically search the spots with power and a view of the room. An app like RF Signal Detector runs the wireless scans from your iPhone in a few minutes.

How much does a professional bug sweep cost?

A professional TSCM sweep of an average home in the US typically runs $1,500 to $6,000, depending on square footage, the equipment used, and whether vehicles and phones are included. It is worth the cost when real stakes are involved, such as litigation or a business dispute, and after your own scans have surfaced something concrete. Be wary of very cheap sweeps that rely on consumer-grade detectors.

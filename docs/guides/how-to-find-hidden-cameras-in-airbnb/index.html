How to find hidden cameras in an Airbnb

Updated July 25, 2026 · RF Signal Detector team

To find hidden cameras in an Airbnb, scan the Wi-Fi network for unknown connected devices, then physically inspect anything with power and a clear view of the bed or bathroom, and finish by sweeping the dark room with a light to catch lens reflections and infrared glow. Airbnb has banned all indoor cameras since April 2024, so any camera you find inside the rental violates policy and, in most places, the law.

Hidden cameras in short-term rentals are rare, but they are not hypothetical. Police cases turn up covert cameras every year, almost always hidden in ordinary objects pointed at the bed or bathroom. A careful guest with a smartphone and ten minutes can find the overwhelming majority of them. This guide follows the same sweep order that technical surveillance countermeasures (TSCM) professionals use, adapted for a rental you just checked into.

What Airbnb actually allows

Start with the rules, because they define what counts as a violation. On April 30, 2024, Airbnb banned all indoor cameras in listings worldwide, including disclosed cameras, cameras in common areas, and cameras that are unplugged or switched off. If it is inside the home and can record video, it is not allowed, period.

Hosts may still use exterior security cameras, doorbell cameras, and noise decibel monitors, but only if they are disclosed in the listing and not pointed at indoor spaces. Read the listing's "safety and property" section before you travel so you know what to expect. Any camera you find indoors, disclosed or not, is a policy violation and quite possibly a crime.

Sweep in this order, not randomly

Most advice is a pile of tips with no sequence. Professionals sweep in a deliberate order because each pass narrows what the next has to cover:

  1. Network first. Scan the Wi-Fi before you unpack. It takes two minutes and covers the whole property at once.
  2. Sightlines second. Stand where you are most exposed, in bed and in the bathroom, and look outward. A camera is only useful if it can see you, so every viable hiding spot lies along a sightline to those positions.
  3. Power third. Trace outlets and permanently powered objects along those sightlines. Battery cameras exist, but most covert cameras live inside something that is always plugged in, because batteries die.
  4. Optics last. Darken the room and sweep with a light to catch lens reflections and infrared glow. This is your safety net for wired cameras that never touch the network.

The rest of this guide takes each pass in turn.

Pass 1: scan the Wi-Fi network

Almost every consumer spy camera streams over Wi-Fi, and the most common models connect to the rental's own network because that is the path of least resistance for the host. Join the Airbnb's Wi-Fi and run a scan that lists every connected device.

Knowing what to look for matters more than the scan itself. Real covert camera device names fall into three patterns:

  • Obvious camera names like "IPCamera," "IPCAM-3421," "HDWifiCamPro," "V380," "Yi Camera," "Wyze Cam," or "Reolink." Cheap spy cameras often ship with the manufacturer's default hostname and hosts rarely rename them.
  • Module names like "ESP32-CAM" or "Hi3518," which are the chipsets inside many DIY and no-brand pinhole cameras.
  • Blank or generic entries with no hostname, just a MAC address. The first half of every MAC address identifies the maker, so a "nameless" device that resolves to Hangzhou Hikvision, Dahua, Shenzhen Reecam, or Espressif (the ESP32 maker) deserves attention even though nothing on the shelf looks like a camera. Expect legitimate devices too: the router itself, a smart TV, a thermostat, smart plugs, maybe a disclosed exterior doorbell. The goal is not to panic at every entry but to account for each one, then investigate the remainder.

One honest caveat: a network scan cannot see a camera on a separate hidden network, on cellular data, or recording to a memory card with no wireless at all. That is why the sweep does not stop here. Pass 2: think in sightlines, then check the classic hosts

Lie on the bed and slowly look around the room. Then stand in the shower and do the same. Every object you can see is a candidate; everything else is not, because a camera buried in a hallway closet records nothing worth the risk. This single trick cuts your search area by more than half.

Pass 3: follow the power

Walk the room and account for every outlet. Covert cameras that run continuously need continuous power, so anomalies cluster around electricity: a cable that disappears behind a mirror, a power strip feeding something you cannot identify, a charger left plugged in that nobody needs. Unplugging a suspicious object for the rest of your stay is a reasonable, low-drama mitigation, with the firm exception of smoke detectors, which you should report rather than disable.

Pass 4: the darkness test for lenses and infrared

The final pass catches what no network scan can: wired and memory-card cameras with no wireless signal at all. It exploits two physical facts about every camera.

First, lenses reflect. Make the room as dark as you can, then sweep your phone flashlight slowly across the sightlines you mapped earlier, holding the light close to your eyes and watching for a small, bright, bluish glint that stays fixed on one point as you move. Glass and lens coatings bounce light straight back at you in a way that plastic and paint do not. Move slowly; at normal speed you will walk right past a pinhole reflection.

Second, most cameras that record in the dark use infrared illuminators, and phone cameras can see infrared even though your eyes cannot. In the dark room, pan your phone's front camera, which usually has the weaker infrared filter, across the room. An active night-vision camera appears as a cluster of faint white or purple glowing dots on your screen. Verify your phone sees infrared by pointing a TV remote at it and pressing a button.

If you find something

Stay calm and resist the urge to grab it. Do this instead:

  1. Do not touch, unplug, or dismantle the device. It is evidence.
  2. Photograph it in place from several angles, with something that establishes the date and the listing.
  3. Move yourself and your belongings out of its view, or cover it with a towel.
  4. Contact local police. Covert recording of private spaces is a criminal offense in most jurisdictions.
  5. Report it to Airbnb through the app within 72 hours. That window preserves your refund and rebooking support.
  6. Leave if you feel unsafe. No booking is worth staying under surveillance.

Keep perspective, and build the habit

The vast majority of hosts are honest, and most sweeps end with a boring, reassuring list of explainable devices. That is a good outcome, not wasted effort. A two-minute network scan on arrival plus one careful pass of the sightlines removes the doubt entirely, and the same method works in hotel rooms too. Whether you run the sweep manually or use an app to speed up the network and lens checks, the core skill is the same: know what belongs in the room, and account for what does not.

Frequently asked questions

Are cameras allowed inside an Airbnb?

No. Since April 30, 2024, Airbnb has banned all indoor cameras in listings, even disclosed ones and even devices that are switched off. Hosts may still use exterior cameras and noise monitors, but they must be disclosed in the listing. Any camera you find inside the rental is a policy violation.

Can my phone really detect hidden cameras?

Yes, for two of the three main detection methods. A phone can scan the Wi-Fi network for connected camera hardware and can spot the infrared glow or lens reflection of a camera with its own camera. What a phone cannot do is detect a wired camera that records to a memory card and never touches the network, which is why a physical inspection is still part of every sweep.

What should I do if I find a hidden camera in my Airbnb?

Do not touch or unplug it. Photograph the device, its location, and anything that shows the date, then move out of its view and contact local police, since covert recording of private spaces is a crime in most jurisdictions. Report it to Airbnb within 72 hours through the app to preserve your refund and rebooking options.

Where are hidden cameras most often found in rentals?

In objects that face the bed or bathroom and have their own power supply. The most common hosts are smoke detectors, alarm clocks, USB chargers and power adapters, Wi-Fi routers, and decorative items on shelves opposite the bed. Anything with a persistent power source and a clear sightline deserves a close look.

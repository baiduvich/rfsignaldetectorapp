How to tell if a smoke detector has a hidden camera

Updated July 25, 2026 · RF Signal Detector team

To tell if a smoke detector has a hidden camera, darken the room and sweep a flashlight across it: a lens returns a small, sharp glint that plain plastic does not. Then view it through your phone's front camera in the dark to spot infrared glow, and check for red flags like a glossy black window, a steady rather than blinking LED, or a unit angled toward the bed instead of flat on the ceiling.

Smoke detectors are the most common disguise for hidden cameras, for a simple reason. A detector sits high on the ceiling with a clear line of sight to the entire room, it is expected in every bedroom, and it already has a plausible excuse for a small light and a plastic grille. The good news: camera units sold as "smoke detector spy cameras" get several details wrong, and once you know what a real detector looks like, the fakes are easy to catch. This guide covers the physical giveaways, the two phone-based tests, and the placement clues that fire code itself hands you.

Why smoke detectors are the favorite hiding spot

Search any large online marketplace for "smoke detector camera" and you will find dozens of ready-made units: a 1080p camera, Wi-Fi streaming, motion detection, and a microSD slot, all packed into a housing molded to look like a household smoke alarm. They cost less than a dinner out and take two minutes to mount, which is why they turn up in rental horror stories far more often than cameras hidden in books or plants.

Two versions exist. The ready-made fake is all camera and usually cannot detect smoke at all. The rarer version is a real detector that someone has opened and fitted with a pinhole camera. The fake is easier to spot because the manufacturer compromised the design to fit the lens, but the same tests catch both.

Know what a real smoke detector looks like

The fastest way to spot a fake is to know the genuine article. Real residential detectors, ionization or photoelectric, share features that camera units routinely lack.

FeatureReal smoke detectorTypical camera unit
Test buttonProminent, clicks, triggers a loud alarmMissing, or present but does nothing
Front surfaceMatte plastic with open vents for smoke entryOften has a glossy dark window, dome, or a single odd pinhole
LED behaviorBrief red or green blink every 30 to 60 secondsSteady glow, no light at all, or a light that reacts when you move
MarkingsBrand (Kidde, First Alert, BRK), model number, UL listing, date stamp on the backNo brand, or a generic label with no certification marks
PositionFlat on the ceiling, or high on a wall near the ceilingAngled, low on a wall, or placed where it "faces" the bed or shower
SoundChirps loudly when the battery runs lowSilent, always

Inside, the differences are starker. A genuine detector contains a sensing chamber (a small round cage that smoke drifts into), a piezo horn for the alarm, and a battery or hardwire connector. A camera unit holds a lens module, a circuit board with an antenna, and usually a microSD slot and a USB charging port. If you ever open a detector in your own home and see a memory card slot, you have your answer. No smoke alarm needs one.

The visual giveaways, from the floor

Before any tests, just look. Stand under the detector and check three details.

An extra hole that does not match the pattern

Real detectors have regular, repeating vent slots so smoke can reach the sensing chamber. A camera needs one clean opening for its lens, so look for a single round pinhole that breaks the pattern, often near the center or along one edge.

A glossy dome or dark window

Camera lenses need an unobstructed view, so many fake units include a smooth, dark, slightly reflective panel or bubble. Real detectors are matte plastic all over. Anything shiny and black deserves a closer look.

The LED acts wrong

A real detector blinks briefly about once a minute to show it has power. A light that glows continuously, reacts when you walk past, or is missing entirely is a red flag. Some camera units also leak a faint red or purple glow in the dark from their infrared emitters, which leads to the next test.

The flashlight test, step by step

The classic lens-reflection check costs nothing.

  1. Close the curtains and turn off every light in the room.
  2. Turn on your phone's flashlight and stand directly under the detector.
  3. Sweep the beam slowly across the detector's face, then move a step to the side and sweep again. Repeat from three or four angles.
  4. Watch for a pinpoint glint, often blue or purple tinted, that stays locked to one exact spot as your angle changes. That is glass lens elements reflecting light back at you.
  5. Compare it against the detector's own LED window, which reflects softly and diffusely. A lens reflection is sharper, smaller, and brighter.

Take your time on step 3. Pinhole lenses are a couple of millimeters wide, and the glint only appears when your light hits them close to head-on.

The phone camera infrared test

Many hidden cameras illuminate the room at night with infrared LEDs, invisible to your eyes but not to your phone's front camera, which usually lacks the infrared filter the rear camera has.

  1. Make the room as dark as you can.
  2. Open your camera app and switch to the selfie camera.
  3. Point it at the detector and watch the screen. Infrared emitters show up as bright white or purple dots.
  4. Calibrate first: point the front camera at a TV remote while pressing a button. If the remote's tip flashes on screen, your phone can see infrared.

One honest caveat: this only catches cameras with active infrared, and many switch it on only in darkness, which is why you darken the room first. A camera without night vision will pass this test, so treat the infrared check as one layer, not the whole answer.

Placement red flags: let fire code do the work

An underused trick: real smoke detectors follow placement rules, hidden cameras follow sight lines. When the two disagree, be suspicious.

  • Bathrooms. Fire safety guidance says detectors should not go in bathrooms or within about 3 feet of a bathroom door, because steam triggers false alarms. A "smoke detector" inside a bathroom, especially facing the shower, has no legitimate reason to be there.
  • Wall-mounted and angled. Real detectors go flat on the ceiling, or high on a wall within about a foot of it. A unit halfway down a wall, or tilted so its face points at the bed, is positioned like a camera, not an alarm.
  • Two detectors in one small room. One per bedroom is standard. If a second unit sits a few feet from an existing one, test it first.
  • Facing the bed. Ceiling-center placement is normal. A detector positioned off-center exactly where it gains a straight view of the bed or changing area deserves the flashlight test before anything else.

None of these proves anything alone. Plenty of landlords install detectors sloppily. But odd placement tells you where to spend your two minutes of testing.

What to do if you find one

If a test comes back positive, resist the urge to rip the unit down. Hidden cameras in spaces where people expect privacy are illegal in every US state, and Airbnb banned all indoor cameras in listings in 2024, disclosed or not. Photograph the device and its position, note the time, and move out of its view. Contact local police, then report it to the platform or your landlord in writing. Leave the device in place, since it may hold evidence, including footage of whoever installed it.

Finally, cameras are only one kind of bug. If someone was willing to plant a lens, an audio device is even cheaper to hide, so it is worth reading how to detect listening devices and making a quick sweep part of your check-in routine anywhere you stay.

Frequently asked questions

Can a smoke detector really hide a camera?

Yes. Fully assembled camera units shaped like smoke detectors are sold openly online for under 50 dollars, and cameras can also be planted inside a genuine detector shell. The disguise works because a ceiling-mounted detector has a clear view of the whole room and nobody thinks twice about one.

Does the flashlight test actually work on smoke detectors?

It works well when done correctly. Darken the room, then sweep a flashlight across the detector slowly from several angles. A camera lens returns a small, sharp glint, often with a blue or purple tint, that stays fixed on one spot as you move. Plastic vents and LED windows reflect dully and diffusely, so the difference is noticeable.

Is it legal for a host or landlord to put a camera in a smoke detector?

No. Hidden cameras in bedrooms and bathrooms are illegal in every US state, and Airbnb has banned all indoor cameras in listings, disclosed or not, since April 2024. If you find one, do not unplug it. Photograph it, leave the room, and contact the police and the platform.

How do I check a smoke detector without taking it off the ceiling?

You do not need a ladder for the first three checks. Do the flashlight sweep from the floor, view the detector through your phone's front camera in a dark room to catch infrared glow, and run a Wi-Fi network scan to see whether any camera-like device is streaming. Only open the unit if it is your own home and your own detector.
